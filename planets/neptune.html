GET TO KNOW THE PLANET

Explore Neptune

Neptune is the eighth planet from the Sun and the most distant recognised planet in our Solar System. It is an ice giant, similar in composition to Uranus.

Its atmosphere consists mainly of hydrogen and helium, with methane contributing to its blue appearance. Beneath the atmosphere lies a deep, hot region containing water, ammonia, and methane under enormous pressure.

Neptune has an active atmosphere with enormous storms and extremely powerful winds. Its most famous historical feature was the Great Dark Spot, a large storm observed by Voyager 2 in 1989.

Did you know?

Neptune takes approximately 165 Earth years to complete one orbit around the Sun. Since its discovery in 1846, it completed its first observed full orbit in 2011.

Neptune's deep-blue appearance and dynamic atmosphere make it one of the most distinctive planets in the Solar System.

WHAT IS INSIDE NEPTUNE?

Internal Structure

Neptune is an ice giant with a layered interior. Its atmosphere gradually becomes denser and hotter towards the centre, eventually reaching an extremely hot, high-pressure interior.

Outer Atmosphere

A gaseous layer made mainly of hydrogen and helium, with methane contributing to Neptune's blue colour.

Icy Mantle

A deep, hot and dense region containing water, ammonia, and methane under extreme pressure.

Central Core

Scientists believe Neptune has a dense central region containing rock and other heavier materials.

A REMARKABLE NEPTUNE FEATURE

Extreme Winds

The Fastest Planetary Winds

Neptune experiences some of the fastest winds measured on any planet in the Solar System. Wind speeds can reach approximately 2,000 kilometres per hour.

These powerful winds help create changing clouds and enormous storms. One famous example was the Great Dark Spot, observed by Voyager 2 in 1989. Unlike Jupiter's Great Red Spot, Neptune's large dark storms can appear and disappear over time.

EXPLORING THE DISTANT ICE GIANT

Neptune Missions

V

Voyager 2

NASA's Voyager 2 spacecraft made its closest approach to Neptune in August 1989. It remains the only spacecraft to have visited the planet.

During its flyby, Voyager 2 studied Neptune's atmosphere, rings, magnetic field, and moons. It also discovered six previously unknown moons and several new rings.
